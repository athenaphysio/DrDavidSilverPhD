Where it began

This project started at
my own kitchen table.

My wife works in HR. My sister is an HR Director. Over years of conversations at the end of long days — in the quiet moments when the professional mask comes off — they showed me something I hadn’t seen clearly before: the people most responsible for organisational wellbeing are often the least supported themselves.

What they described wasn’t complaint. It was something more interesting — a picture of people who carry enormous emotional and organisational weight, frequently in silence, while spending their days building systems to support everyone around them. People who are, in the truest sense, the most overlooked employees in the building.

As a Chartered Physiotherapist with a PhD in research and 20 years of clinical practice, I understand what sustained occupational stress actually does to a human body and mind. I also understand what it takes to help someone feel genuinely seen. Those two things together felt like the foundation for something worth building properly.

The HR Wellbeing Research Project began in 2026 — a series of in-depth qualitative interviews with senior HR professionals, conducted with their trust and on the explicit understanding that their experiences would be treated with complete confidentiality and used only to inform work that would, in time, reach and help people in their position.

Methodology

Qualitative — in-depth interviews

Semi-structured interviews of 30–45 minutes, conducted with senior HR professionals across multiple sectors. All participants anonymous. Thematic analysis applied to identify patterns across the data. Findings will be published as a peer-informed white paper in 2027.

Emerging findings

What the data
is already showing.

The research is ongoing and findings are preliminary — the white paper will present the full analysis. The themes below represent patterns that have emerged consistently across interviews conducted to date.

01

The paradox of invisible support

HR professionals are expected to provide and facilitate support for the entire workforce while operating with little or no equivalent structure for themselves. The expectation of resilience appears to be embedded in the role identity itself, making it difficult to acknowledge personal struggle.

02

Performative wellbeing creates additional burden

Participants describe the experience of designing, launching and championing wellbeing programmes for colleagues while personally experiencing the conditions those programmes are designed to address. The cognitive dissonance is significant and rarely acknowledged.

03

Leadership visibility amplifies isolation

Senior HR professionals report that their proximity to leadership and their role as trusted confidants creates a particular form of professional isolation — one in which candour about personal experience is perceived as a professional risk.

04

Physical responses go unacknowledged

Sustained occupational stress produces consistent somatic responses — disrupted sleep, muscular tension, altered appetite, reduced movement — that participants describe clearly but rarely connect to their working conditions. The physical dimension of HR stress appears largely invisible within the profession.

05

The ‘empty cup’ phenomenon

Multiple participants used spontaneous metaphors of depletion — giving from an empty cup, running on nothing — to describe sustained periods at work. The language is consistent and suggests a shared phenomenology that has not been formally documented in the HR wellbeing literature.

06

Trust as the primary enabler

Where participants had experienced genuinely effective support, the common factor was not the programme or the resource — it was a person whom they trusted and who asked. The interpersonal dimension of support appears to matter far more than the structural one.

These findings are preliminary and drawn from a subset of the total interviews planned. The full white paper will present complete thematic analysis, participant demographics and methodological detail.

Participate in the research

Are you a senior HR professional?
Your experience matters.

The research is built on the generosity of HR professionals who are willing to speak honestly about their experience of the role. Interviews take 30–45 minutes and can be conducted in person or remotely. All participants remain anonymous and are offered the opportunity to review any content before it is used.

Participation is entirely voluntary. There is no obligation to discuss anything you are not comfortable with, and the conversation can stop at any point. David conducts all interviews personally — they are not delegated or processed through a third party.

Confidentiality

Nothing will be attributed to you personally. No identifying detail will appear in any published material without your explicit agreement. Verbal consent is sufficient — no forms, no data processing beyond the anonymised research record.
