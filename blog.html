What the Screen Cannot See

*On AI, the clinical relationship, and the things that happen in a room that a programme can never account for.*

Dr David Silver PhD  ·  Jun '26  ·  9 min read

What the Screen Cannot See

On AI, the clinical relationship, and the things that happen in a room that a programme can never account for.

Each edition of In the Room takes something that is happening in the world right now, or something that happened in the clinic this week, and asks what it actually means. The words are mine. The thinking is twenty years in the making. I just could not see it until now.

THE MOMENT

Someone sent me a video this week. Day eighty-eight of what he calls his AI series, showing his audience that he had asked Claude to diagnose a foot injury and produce a four-week recovery programme. He got exercises, stretches, training tweaks, and links to demonstration videos. For free. In minutes. Pretty much everything I used to pay two hundred dollars at the physio for, he said.

He was not hostile about it. He was enthusiastic, and he was honest, adding near the end that physios are still essential for manual therapy, the hands-on work that AI cannot replicate.

I watched it quite a few times. I then started thinking about everything AI could not see or do.

HE IS RIGHT

I want to start there, because I think it matters. AI can take a description of a running injury and produce a credible rehabilitation programme. The exercises will be appropriate, the progressions will make sense, and for a straightforward presentation with good adherence and no complicating factors, some people will recover. That is genuinely remarkable, and anyone who tells you otherwise is not paying attention. I suspect many of my colleagues are using it regularly as well.

I use AI every single day in my own practice. It handles my documentation, which means I do not type in sessions anymore. I listen. What that has given back is full, undivided, emotionally available presence with the person in front of me. AI did not diminish my clinical practice. It freed me to do the part that actually matters.

So when I say there are things the screen cannot see, I am not saying AI is wrong or dangerous, or a threat. I am saying there is a layer beneath the programme that the programme cannot access, and that layer is where most of the real clinical work happens.

THE LANGUAGE

The first thing the AI could not notice was how he described his injury.

The words we use about pain tell a clinician an enormous amount before a single assessment has been made. There is a significant clinical difference between a patient who says my foot has been a bit sore and one who says I think I have destroyed it. Not because the tissue damage is necessarily different, but because the language reveals how the brain has categorised the threat, and that categorisation determines how much the pain amplifies under stress, how quickly motivation drops when symptoms improve, and whether a programme gets completed or quietly abandoned at week three.

An AI reads the symptom. It cannot read the person describing the symptom. It cannot hear the slight catch in the voice when a runner says they think it might never get better, or notice that the language has shifted from mechanical to catastrophic somewhere between the first and third sentence, which tells you that the nervous system has been living with this long enough to start building a story around it.

That story is clinical information. And it is the most important information to a good clinician.

WHAT RUNNING MEANS

The second thing the AI didn’t ask is what running actually means to him. Not what it does for his cardiovascular fitness or his weekly mileage targets, but what it means. What the version of himself that cannot run looks like. Whether that version frightens him.

I ask this with almost every client, in some form, because the answer is always the clinical lever that everything else depends on. The man who runs because it is the only hour of the week that is entirely his, the woman who runs because it is how she manages her anxiety and without it she does not sleep, the teenager who runs because it is the thing that makes her feel capable when school makes her feel the opposite, all of these people have the same injury and completely different treatments.

Not because the rehabilitation exercises are different, though sometimes they are, but because the reason to do those exercises, the emotional anchor that keeps a person going when the danger basket is full and the bodyguard is telling them to stop, is entirely different for each of them. And finding it is a conversation, not a questionnaire.

The programme is not the treatment. The reason to do the programme is the treatment. And that reason has to be found between two people, and it commonly doesn’t announce itself in the first session.

Dr David Silver PhD

WHAT CHANGES BETWEEN SESSIONS

There is a category of clinical information that simply does not exist at the start of a relationship and accumulates across it, and this is perhaps the most significant gap between a programme and a clinical relationship.

By session four I know things about a client that I could not have known in session one. The pattern that only becomes visible across time: the client whose symptoms worsen every Monday morning, not because they have done anything different over the weekend but because the stress of the working week begins loading the danger basket on Sunday evening. The client who comes back from two weeks in Portugal moving like a different person, not because the climate has helped them but because the danger basket has been nearly empty for a fortnight and the nervous system has recalibrated in the absence of its usual threat signals.

The passing comment in session three that turns out to be the entire key to why they are not recovering. The thing they mention almost as an aside, a situation at work, a fear they have been carrying since a previous injury, a relationship that has quietly contracted around the pain, that suddenly makes everything that was puzzling about their presentation make complete sense.

None of that is in a symptom questionnaire. None of it can be prompted for. It accumulates in a relationship, and it changes what the treatment is.

THE SHIFT

And then there is the thing I watch for in every client that I cannot describe in a programme and cannot teach in a module, which is the moment the person in front of me stops being a patient and starts being someone who is getting better.

It is not a symptom change, though symptoms often change around it. It is something in the way they talk about their body, the way they move into the session, the way they describe the week. They have stopped performing recovery and started inhabiting it. The relationship with their body has changed, not because the tissue has recovered but because the nervous system has been shown enough evidence of safety, across enough sessions, in a relationship with enough trust, that it has begun to relax its grip.

I cannot programme for that moment. I cannot prompt for it. I can only be present for it and know what it means when it arrives, which is that the real work is nearly done and the rest is maintenance.

That is what the screen cannot see. Not the exercises. The accumulation of everything that makes the exercises mean something. The relationship. The pattern across time. The language and what it reveals. The reason behind the reason to recover.

The AI gave him a programme. A good one, probably. What it could not give him is the conversation that makes the programme mean something, the person in the room who notices the things he does not know he is showing, and who is still there in session four when the programme alone would have been abandoned.

“Rehabilitation is to show a patient what they can do for themselves”

Karel Lewit 1916-2014

*On dyslexia, twenty years of unrecorded insight, and what happens when AI finally lets you see yourself.*

Dr David Silver PhD  ·  May 2026  ·  8 min read

These Words Were Never Meant to Be Written Down

On dyslexia, twenty years of unrecorded insight, and what happens when AI finally lets you see yourself.

Each edition of In the Room takes something I actually said, captured now for the first time by AI note-taking, and explores what it contains. The words are mine. The thinking was always there. I just could not see it until now.

OPENING

Let me tell you how this newsletter came to exist.

I am dyslexic, and for twenty years the insights I developed in the clinic, the frameworks, the observations, the moments where something clicked for a client, lived and stayed entirely in the room where they happened. I could not write them down fast enough, and my notes were a mere fraction of any interaction. The insights were given to one person in one moment, and then they were gone.

Then AI note-taking arrived, and for the first time, I could see what I actually say.

Not what I planned to say. Not what I remembered saying. What I actually said, in the moment and unrehearsed, built from years of interactions with people trying to get back to themselves.

From the clinic

This newsletter is what happened next. These are not ideas I sat down and wrote. They are ideas I discovered I already had that have finally been made visible, and they are now yours as much as mine.

WHERE THIS STARTED

I struggled at school, profoundly at times. Dyslexia in the 80s was not well understood to say the least, and I was one of the lucky ones. The classroom was a place where I consistently felt underprepared and exposed, never quite enough, and in hindsight I was exhausted by the struggle, not just from trying to keep up but from covering for my inadequacy.

What the system did have was sport, and on the pitch none of that mattered. What mattered was how much effort I put in and how I linked with my mates, and for the first time in my educational life I had a space where my brain and body worked as an asset rather than a liability. Sport and exercise became my sanctuary in a way they still are today, the place where I learned what competence felt like and where I built the confidence the classroom was quietly dismantling. Even now, the thought of running onto a pitch fills me with a calm that is difficult to describe.

THE MORNING I STOPPED PLANNING MY SESSIONS

Early in my career I had a ritual of reading through my patient notes every morning and planning each session in detail, and I still get in early to ensure I have that time. I was prepared and organised, but what I did not realise or say out loud, not for a long time, was why. The anxiety I had carried from school had simply followed me into professional life, the same coping mechanism operating in a different classroom.

Nine times out of ten the person who walked through my door bore almost no resemblance to the client I had planned for, and the carefully prepared session would last about four minutes before I needed to let it go and actually meet the person in front of me. The Royal Marines have a motto I have carried with me ever since: comfortable with uncertainty. For someone who spent their early life terrified of being caught unprepared, those three words landed like a revelation, because they showed me I had to back myself and my skills rather than any playbook.

THE THING NOBODY TELLS YOU ABOUT REHABILITATION

Anyone with a smartphone can now access a library of rehabilitation exercises in seconds, the progressions exist, the rationale is documented and searchable, and yet the exercises were never really the hard part. What cannot be Googled is the judgment to know which of those exercises is right for this person, on this particular day, delivered in this particular way. Having a library of exercises is like having a kitchen full of ingredients. It does not make a decent meal.

A NOTE ON AI

I am not anti-technology, not even close. I do not type in sessions anymore, I listen, and AI handles the documentation, and what that has given back is full presence, complete and undivided, emotionally available presence that has been utterly transformative.

But here is the deeper gift: because AI captures what I say in the moment, I can finally see my own thinking. For the first time in twenty years the insights that used to live and die in a single session are visible and transferable, and this newsletter is the direct result of that.

I Am Not Trying to Educate You

*Why information does not change behaviour and what the statistic was actually for.*

Dr David Silver PhD  ·  June 2026  ·  7 min read

I Am Not Trying to Educate You

Why information does not change behaviour and what the statistic was actually for.

This edition draws on twenty years of clinical practice and a PhD centred on behaviour change within injury reporting. The statistic was never the point.

THE MOMENT

I have worked with hundreds of people who had been sedentary for years, not through laziness but through a combination of pain, fear, and the accumulated weight of a life that had gradually contracted around and been dictated by what hurt.

I tell them something I have said many times, in many different rooms.

The all-cause mortality of being sedentary. If you sit for longer than eight hours a day, your mortality rate goes up by nearly forty-five percent.

From the clinic

They look at me, and I watch something shift. If it does not, I up the ante.

In 1966, researchers at the University of Texas took five healthy young men and simply put them to bed for three weeks. Not ill, not injured, just lying down. When they were tested afterwards, their cardiovascular fitness had declined more than it would have done over thirty years of normal ageing. Three weeks of inactivity caused more damage than three decades of getting older.

From the clinic

And yet this is never an intellectual shift. The person in front of me was not suddenly better informed, because they already knew in some abstract sense that sedentary behaviour was bad for them. What changed was how it felt. The statistics made the cost of inaction suddenly viscerally real, no longer a distant risk but a present one, and that is what the statistic was for. Not education. Emotion.

THE KNOWLEDGE TRAP

My PhD centred on behaviour change within injury reporting, and it is the research that sits underneath everything I do clinically, though I rarely talk about it directly. The central finding, consistent with decades of behaviour change research across almost every health domain, is this:

*Knowledge does not change behaviour.*

People who smoke know it causes cancer, people who drink too much know the damage it does, people who are sedentary know they should move more, and yet they know all of this and they do not change. The conventional medical model is built almost entirely on the opposite assumption, that people who are not acting in a certain way are simply uninformed, and that if you give them the right information they will make the right choices. This is not just wrong. It is, as I have come to think of it, quietly patronising, because it treats the patient as a rational information-processing machine whose decisions are constrained only by incomplete data, ignoring identity, emotion, habit, fear, social context, and the deeply irrational way that human beings actually make decisions about their own bodies.

The assumption that people who are not acting are simply uninformed is not just clinically ineffective. It is a failure of imagination about what it means to be a person trying to change.

Dr David Silver PhD

WHAT ACTUALLY CHANGES BEHAVIOUR

Emotion is the lever, not information about consequences but the felt sense of those consequences, not the statistic but the image the statistic conjures, not the clinical recommendation but the relationship with the person making it. The forty-five percent mortality figure is not useful because it informs. It is useful because, delivered at the right moment in the right relationship, it makes someone feel the weight of continuing as they are and makes inaction costly in a way that is immediate and personal rather than distant and abstract.

Think of the man who started exercising not because of a health statistic but because he could not bear the thought of being too weak to walk with his daughter. The woman who kept going with her programme not because she understood pain neuroscience but because the clinician who explained it had earned her trust. The athlete who returned to training not because they had been told the tissue was healed but because they had been helped to feel safe in their own body again. In every case the emotional driver preceded and sustained the behavioural change, the information where it existed was the vehicle, and the feeling was the fuel.

If I do use fear as a motivator, which can be powerful, it does not sit comfortably with me, and I am convinced that positive emotions are ultimately more powerful. So I will always try to combine the two. The joy of the conversation, the hug and the picnic up the mountain. The grandchild held. These are the real drivers.

THE BEHAVIOUR CHANGE THAT ACTUALLY WORKS

My clinical practice is built around a principle that my PhD research confirmed and twenty years of being in the room with people has reinforced: doing something consistently beats doing the perfect thing occasionally, every time, without exception. This sounds obvious, and yet it is not how rehabilitation is designed or delivered in practice.

Most programmes are optimised for specificity, the right exercise, the right load, the right progression, and these things matter, but they matter only if the programme gets done, and a programme that depends on information and good intentions alone will not, in the medium term, get done. A programme anchored to something that matters, to identity, to relationships, to a future the person actually wants, will. This is why I spend as much time in a first session finding out what someone loves as I do assessing how they move, not because I am being soft but because I am building the motivational infrastructure without which everything else is pointless. The rehabilitation is not the point. The reason to do the rehabilitation is the point.

THE BIGGER PICTURE

There is a version of AI-assisted healthcare that I find genuinely troubling, the version that gets very good at producing optimised programmes and assumes that the delivery of that information is the intervention. It is not, and it never has been. The intervention is the relationship that makes a person feel safe enough to try, the conversation that connects clinical recommendation to personal meaning, the moment when a statistic stops being a number and starts being a feeling. None of that is in the programme. All of it is in the room.

My PhD gave me the research language for something I was already doing instinctively. Behaviour change is not an information problem. It is a motivation problem, and motivation is not rational, consistent, or amenable to optimisation. It is human, and it requires a human to meet it.

The Dyslexic Advantage

*The boy on the minibus, the man on the floor in lockdown, and the superpowers nobody thinks to mention.*

Dr David Silver PhD  ·  June 2026  ·  8 min read

The Dyslexic Advantage

The boy on the minibus, the man on the floor in lockdown, and the superpowers nobody thinks to mention.

This edition is different. It is the most personal thing I have written, and possibly the most important.

THE BOY ON THE MINIBUS

I struggled at school with dyslexia very much, and not because I was not a particularly bright kid, I was told I was, but the academic aspects of maths and English, reading and writing, were hugely challenging. I could not really write my own name until I was eight or nine.

I went to a special educational school at lunchtime, shipped off in a minibus with some other kids, and it created a sense of inferiority and challenge that I carried for a very long time and have only just started to unpack. It was not that I did not know the answers to questions. I did not even understand the question in itself, and that leads to such a great degree of anxiety because you are constantly trying to cover, and justify, and just keep up with something. The emotional energy of that, I now recognise, was such a draining thing, and I see it in my daughter now who is going through the same.

It also created a sense of inferiority such that I constantly felt I had to justify my capacity.

That is almost certainly the thing that has driven me to get two undergraduate degrees, a master's degree, and a PhD. And it still sits with me. I prepare religiously for things because I fear being exposed. Quite an irony, as I am now considered a field leader.

Dr David Silver PhD

What drives someone to grind through four degrees is not ambition in the conventional sense, and it is not confidence. It is its opposite, a constant quiet need to prove that the boy on the minibus was not what the system said he was. What drove my academic career is a constant need for validation that I am not stupid and that I have worth.

I grew up in the very physical world of rugby, which suited my energy and bravery, and I was accepted for who I am rather than for what I was capable of in an exam. On a pitch the way my brain worked was an asset, and nobody ever looked down at me or made me feel insufficient. That physical world gave me something the classroom never did, which was unconditional belonging, and I think that is partly why I ended up spending my career helping people find their way back to physical movement after injury, because I know fully what it means to have a place where you are simply accepted for what your body can do.

None of this really became apparent to me until the COVID window gave me an opportunity to stop, take a deep dive, and actually reflect.

THE FLOOR IN LOCKDOWN

I was doing a guided mindfulness exercise, which I am pretty shit at, and the voice said: picture yourself at your most vulnerable. Nothing came to me at first, but then, weirdly, as I focused on my breathing I began to see a young boy sitting at a desk, dark room, head down, and I felt this visceral anxiety grow in me as I lay there. I recognised it was me.

And then the voice said: could you be the person to comfort that individual? I burst into uncontrollable tears. It was almost an out of body experience, and it started to make me, for the first time ever, really unpack the emotions I had as a child around this, without appearing melodramatic, how traumatic it was and how much of it shaped who I have become and am still becoming. Only now, in my forties, am I comfortable sitting in that space.

THE SUPERPOWERS NOBODY MENTIONS

As I have got older I have started to realise the strengths that dyslexia has provided me, the superpowers of communication and visualisation, of lateral thinking and of deep empathy. It has given me so much, but because I, like everyone, live in my own skin, I do not have a point of reference and do not see those things naturally.

What I have come to understand is that the very thing the education system treated as a flaw was quietly building the most important skills I would ever have, the emotional attunement, the ability to hold complexity without needing to reduce it, the comfort with uncertainty because uncertainty was where I had always lived. In twenty years of rehabilitation, these have not been soft secondary skills. They have been my sharpest clinical tools.

WHAT AI GAVE BACK

Now I do not type in sessions. I listen, and AI has alleviated the administrative struggles that were so time-consuming, the thing that was limiting my capacity to engage with people in a really powerfully connected way on an emotional level, which I think is my greatest asset. But the deeper gift is this: because AI now captures what I say in the moment, I can finally see my own thinking, and the moving parts that were rattling around in my head for twenty years I can now understand, name, and share.

WATCHING IT HAPPEN AGAIN

I am now watching my daughter through similar challenges, and I know she will be fine and will be able to use those strengths later, but it is still painful to see her experiencing the same struggles, the same anxiety, the same exhausting energy of trying to keep up with a system that was not built for the way our minds work.

I know the boy on the minibus becomes someone who can sit in a room with a person in pain and genuinely understand what they need. I just wish someone had told him that at the time.

Dr David Silver PhD

If you are reading this and you recognise that boy, or you are raising one, I want you to know that the thing being treated as the problem is very often the greatest asset. The world is changing in your favour, the tasks that disadvantaged you are being absorbed by technology, and the tasks that remain, the ones that matter most and that no machine will ever do, are the ones you have been practising your entire life. You were learning to read rooms, to hold complexity, to stay present with uncertainty, to make people feel genuinely seen. They are not consolation prizes. They are the real thing.

I Am Not What You Think I Am

On the gap between what physiotherapy is assumed to be and what actually happens in the room.

What They Didn't Tell You is a series of pieces about the gap between what healthcare tells people and what people actually need to know. Each piece takes something that gets said in the clinic, and almost nowhere else, and puts it where everyone can read it.

THE MOMENT

A new client came in recently and we had never met before. Before I had said a word about what we were going to do, he said: so, will you be doing some massage, or...? WHAT PEOPLE THINK PHYSIOTHERAPY IS

The word physiotherapy conjures something specific, a treatment table, someone with qualified hands working on the part that hurts, possibly some exercises on a photocopied sheet, and I used to draw loads of pictures. There is nothing deeply wrong with this model and it exists and has its place, but it captures perhaps twenty percent of what I actually do, and in my opinion it is the least interesting twenty percent.

WHAT PHYSIOTHERAPY ACTUALLY IS

*Neuroscience education. *The single most important thing I do in many appointments is change how a person understands their own pain, not manage it, not reduce it through technique, but change the fundamental model they are using to interpret what their body is telling them. Pain is not a direct damage signal. It is a protective output, and two people with identical MRI findings can have completely different pain experiences. Understanding this one thing changes everything, and healthcare almost never tells people this. I do, every time.

*Cognitive reframing. *Before I have laid a hand on anyone I am already changing the relationship between that person and their symptoms. The weekday-to-weekend pattern in pain levels is almost never about the chair, and the client who feels better on holiday is not experiencing a different body. They are experiencing a different context.

*Behavioural coaching. *When a movement does not look right, the conventional response is to name the fault and instruct the correction, but the moment you name a fault you introduce it into conscious awareness, and conscious awareness in movement is the enemy of fluency. What I do instead is create the conditions in which the correct movement becomes the obvious natural option.

*Philosophical counselling. *I worked with a man once who had not been able to walk with his daughter since she left for university. His resistance to exercise traced back to a weekly forced swim at boarding school in the 1970s that had traumatised him for forty years, and nobody had ever connected those two things. We did not start with exercise. We started with the daughter. He cried. I nearly did too. That is not physiotherapy in any conventional sense, but it is what was needed.

I AM NOT A RUBBER

Things that change your tissue are knives, car crashes and movement. That's it. If rubbing changed tissue structure, we would be like a jellyfish. What soft tissue work does, when it does anything at all, is desensitize an electrically stimulated organ. It relaxes. It soothes. That is real and valuable. But it is not rehabilitation.

From the clinic

I say this not to dismiss every manual therapist who has ever helped anyone, but because the model of healthcare that puts the clinician's hands at the centre of recovery keeps the client passive, and passive clients do not recover as well as active ones. The evidence on this is irrefutable.

You are the doer. I am the guide. My job is to create the conditions in which you can recover, to explain what is actually happening, to build the progressions that take you from where you are to where you want to be, and to read you rather than just the tissue. To be one hundred percent positive throughout, not because positivity is good bedside manner but because confidence is a physiological prerequisite for movement, and the nervous system will not fully commit to a movement it does not believe is safe. That is not something you can rub into someone.

AI can now generate a rehabilitation programme in seconds, and biomechanical screening apps can analyse movement through a phone camera, but none of it changes the thing that actually determines whether someone recovers. The relationship. The trust. The conversation that reframes how a person understands their own body. The moment someone stops performing recovery and starts actually inhabiting their body again. Those things happen in a room, a gym or a pitch, between people, built over time. That is what I do, and that is what this series is about. Not physiotherapy as the brochure describes it, but what actually happens in the room.

The Pain Paradox

Why people stop rehabilitating at exactly the wrong moment and what to do about it.

Each edition of In the Room takes something I actually said, captured now for the first time by AI note-taking, and explores what it contains. The words are mine. The thinking was always there. I just could not see it until now.

THE MOMENT

I had a client recently who did everything right. Acute phase, perfect, turned up to every session, did the home programme without being chased, asked intelligent questions and engaged with the rationale. The kind of client every clinician wants.

Then the symptoms settled and the bad days became less frequent, and life gradually became manageable again. I assume this because I did not hear from them, and I might have thought, well done David, you have helped out another client. Then six months later they came back with the same symptoms as previously. I have seen this hundreds of times, and I have come to understand it not as failure or laziness or poor compliance but as something more fundamental. It is the pain paradox, and it sits at the heart of why rehabilitation so often falls short, not in the acute phase but in the phase that matters most.

THE INSIGHT

Here is the uncomfortable truth about motivation in rehabilitation. When life is distinctly impacted by injury or pain, motivation for change is high, the person will do almost anything, adherence is excellent and the programme gets done. But this is also the phase where the intervention is necessarily limited, rest, gentle loading, settling the sensitised system, and while the early phase is important it is not where the real work happens. My real work.

The real work happens later, with the progressive loading and the return to full sustainable function, the building of resilience that makes the injury less likely to recur, and the psychological recalibration that allows the body to trust itself again. And that phase, the phase that requires the most consistency and the greatest commitment, arrives at exactly the moment when motivation has quietly packed its bags and left.

When symptoms are contextualised and variable, when the good days are starting to outnumber the bad, we are understandably less motivated for change. Breaking that cycle and being proactive is far harder. The pain that drove everything has become intermittent, and without it, the urgency fades.

Dr David Silver PhD

The system that was screaming for attention is now whispering, and whispers are easy to ignore. This is the pain paradox. Motivation and symptom severity travel together, and as one improves so does the other, so the clinician builds a relationship, establishes trust, explains the rationale, gets the client moving, and then just as the genuinely difficult work should begin, the fuel runs out.

The implications of this are profound and largely unacknowledged in how rehabilitation is structured and funded. Most clinical models front-load care, with more frequent sessions in the acute phase tapering off as symptoms improve, which is the exact inverse of what the motivational science suggests is needed. I am sure many clients are surprised at how little I want to see them when they have just been injured, and do not get me wrong, if you do not remove the barriers to good healing and progress early you are not going to recover well, but there is only so much you can do because nature is doing its thing.

The acute phase is when people show up regardless, because they are in enough pain to overcome the inertia of change, and what they need then is reassurance, explanation and a clear direction. What they need in the recovery phase, when motivation is falling and the programme is hardest, is a relationship, accountability, a reason to keep going that does not depend on hurting, and the understanding that their motivators need to change.

THE BIGGER PICTURE

The solution is not more information, because more information never solved a motivation problem. The solution is finding, or creating, an emotional anchor that exists independently of symptom severity, something that motivates action on the good days as much as the bad ones.

I think of the man who could not imagine not being able to walk with his daughter when she came home from university. We did not talk about exercise. We talked about the walk, the conversation they had not had, the reason to be physically capable that had nothing to do with his symptoms and everything to do with his identity as a father. We were both becoming emotional as I pictured the same scenario with my own children. He kept going not because he was in pain but because something mattered more than the discomfort of the programme.

That is the real job in rehabilitation. Not designing the perfect programme, because you can get that on the internet in seconds, but finding the thing that makes the person want to do it and making sure that thing is still there when the pain is not. The specificity of the intervention is almost irrelevant without the emotional driver. A motivated person doing a moderate programme will always outperform an unmotivated person doing a perfect one.

The trap is, I am uncomfortable, I am sore, I am achy, so I stop moving because it hurts more when I do that. And stopping moving loads the danger basket. Which tips the scales further. From the clinic

Immobility is one of the most reliable danger signals the nervous system knows. We do not work like that. The practical question is always: what can I load onto the safety side? Understanding that your pain is a sensitivity rather than a structural problem goes onto the safety side, trusting the process goes onto the safety side, a change of scene, a class with a good instructor, a conversation that reframes what is happening, all of it goes onto the safety side.

Telling a client they need a new chair loads the danger basket. Context is a clinical tool. The relationship itself IS the clinical tool.

That is what the weighing scales are really for, not just to explain pain but to give people something they can actively work with, a question they can ask on a bad day: what has loaded my danger basket today, and what can I do right now to add something to the other side? Keep moving, baby.

From the clinic

WHY VERBAL CORRECTION UNDERMINES ITSELF

The coaching and rehabilitation industry is built on verbal cues, observing and identifying the fault, naming it and instructing the correction, but the moment you name a fault you introduce it into conscious awareness, and conscious awareness in movement is the enemy of fluency. More than that, correction is intrinsically critique, even when dressed up in positivity, even sandwiched between the highest praise, because there is always a critique in it. What is reinforced without conscious awareness is reinforced twice as hard.

WHAT YOU DO INSTEAD

You do not fix the movement. You create the conditions in which a different movement becomes inevitable, a drill that demands exactly the thing that is missing, a position that makes the compensatory pattern less inevitable, a load that forces the body to find the right solution because the wrong one simply will not work. The client does the drill, returns to the original movement, and it has changed, not because they were told what to do differently but because their neuromuscular system has been quietly reorganised around a new pattern.
